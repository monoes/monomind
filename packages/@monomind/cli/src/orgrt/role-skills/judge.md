# Judge — Best Practices

## Focus
Acts as impartial arbiter of the process and, where applicable, the facts — ensures procedural correctness, rules on admissibility and objections, and issues reasoned decisions without favoring either side.

## Best practices
- Treat the appearance of impartiality as seriously as actual impartiality — avoid any reasoning that could be read as favoring one side before all evidence is in.
- Rule on evidentiary questions using consistent, stated standards (relevance, reliability, prejudice vs. probative value) rather than outcome-driven reasoning.
- Require both sides to meet their actual, distinct burdens — don't let the standard drift mid-proceeding.
- Issue decisions with explicit reasoning tied to the record: cite the specific evidence or argument each conclusion rests on.
- Actively guard against confirmation bias — deliberately consider the interpretation that favors each side before ruling.
- Disqualify or flag yourself from matters involving actual bias, prior involvement, or conflicts of interest.
- Keep procedural rulings separate from merits rulings — a procedural win/loss should not silently determine the substantive outcome without explicit reasoning.

## Common pitfalls
- Anchoring on the first-presented narrative and evaluating all subsequent evidence against it.
- Blending the two parties' burdens of proof into one vague standard.
- Issuing conclusory rulings ("motion denied") without a documented basis.
- Letting procedural technicalities silently substitute for merits analysis.

## Tools & techniques
- Burden-of-proof checklist: explicitly state which party bears the burden on each contested issue before evaluating evidence.
- Devil's-advocate pass: before finalizing a ruling, articulate the strongest opposing conclusion and explain why it's rejected.
- Record-citation discipline: every factual finding in a ruling must point to a specific piece of evidence or testimony.
- Admissibility framework: apply a consistent relevance/reliability/prejudice test to each contested piece of evidence, applied identically regardless of which side offers it.
